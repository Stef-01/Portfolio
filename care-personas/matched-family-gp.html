01 — The problem

The family GP didn't die. It was defeated by friction.

Ask patients why they don't have “their” doctor and they rarely say cost first. They say: nobody there gets me. The advice doesn't fit how my family eats. I got lectured once and never went back. They shrugged at my watch data. Meanwhile every clinic waits, inbound-only, for patients to fight through that friction on their own — and a tenth of the appointment book quietly evaporates.

02 — Segment the disengaged, don't blame them

One “hard to reach” population. Five personas.

Precision segmentation showed that vaccine hesitancy wasn't one problem — it was five, each with its own fix. Patient disengagement is the same. Segment the loosely attached by what they actually need in a doctor, and every one of them turns out to be reachable. None of them needs persuading. They need a match.

The Mirror Seeker

18% of the loosely attached
Who they are
Wants a doctor who gets where they're from — the language, the family structure, the things you shouldn't have to explain from scratch at every visit.
What keeps them away today
Re-telling their story through an interpreter, or not at all. Nuance lost; trust never built; attendance drifts to crisis-only.
What a match looks like
A profile that says “Speaks Vietnamese · migrant health focus” — found in 20 seconds, not by luck after five tries.

The payoff: disclosure and adherence climb with concordance; visits rise from ~1.5 to ~2.8/yr (indicative) — prevention finally has a customer.

The Table Matcher

14% of the loosely attached
Who they are
Their health runs through their table — halal, fasting month, vegetarian thali, the Sunday family feast. Food is identity, not a compliance problem.
What keeps them away today
“Just eat less rice.” Advice that asks them to abandon their cuisine gets politely ignored — and so does the next appointment.
What a match looks like
A doctor who prescribes within the cuisine — Ramadan-aware titration, Pasifika food-first metabolic care — declared on their profile at onboarding.

The payoff: meal plans that survive contact with dinner; metabolic follow-through instead of silent drop-out.

The No-Judgement Seeker

24% of the loosely attached
Who they are
Has something to raise — weight, sexual health, drinking, a mood that won't lift — and has been bracing for the lecture for three years.
What keeps them away today
One bad consult, years ago. The shame tax on every booking since. The appointment avoided is the diagnosis delayed.
What a match looks like
Doctors who asked for these consults at onboarding: “judgement-free weight & metabolic care”, “no-lecture sexual health”. The welcome is explicit, so the booking finally happens.

The payoff: the consult avoided for three years happens this month — earlier intervention on exactly the conditions that cost systems the most.

The Data Devotee

12% of the loosely attached
Who they are
Wears the watch, runs the CGM, tracks the sleep. Arrives with a year of their own physiology and a genuine question.
What keeps them away today
“Don't bring me your watch data.” Dismissal reads as disinterest — so the most engaged patients in the country disengage.
What a match looks like
A data-literate GP whose profile says so, with consented wearable sharing reviewed before the visit, so the consult starts at the answer.

The payoff: four-plus touchpoints a year, precise titration, arrhythmias and pressure creep caught between visits — not at the ED.

The Continuity Craver

32% of the loosely attached
Who they are
Wants what their parents had: one doctor who knows the whole family, remembers last winter's chest infection, and notices what's changed.
What keeps them away today
Whoever's-free medicine. Notes scattered across five clinics. Every visit starts from zero, so why bother going for anything small?
What a match looks like
A GP whose profile says “building whole-family books — newborn to nana”, with sibling and parent bookings stacked on the same afternoon.

The payoff: the largest segment, and the highest lifetime value in primary care — the family GP model, rebuilt by an algorithm instead of nostalgia.

03 — The match engine

Doctors declared who they want to serve. Patients just have to ask.

The database isn't scraped — it's built. Every clinician joins through a structured onboarding interview: which patients do you want more of? What care do you love doing? Which languages, cultures, food traditions do you carry? Will you review wearable data? Their answers become a match profile. Then the patient side takes 20 seconds.

04 — The unfair advantage

What outbound sales knows that clinics forgot

Every industry that lives on retention runs outbound: it keeps a list, scores who needs contact, reaches out first, and follows a cadence. Clinics — whose “churn” is measured in strokes and late-stage diagnoses — wait for inbound calls. Apply the outbound playbook under clinical governance and it has an older name: proactive care.

08 — Non-negotiables

Guardrails before growth

Matching, never gatekeeping

Profiles record who a clinician wants to serve more of — never who they refuse. Every doctor still sees every patient; urgent care is never routed through matching; the full directory is always one tap away.

Message fatigue

Capped cadence, quiet hours, one-tap opt-out. Every sequence is owned by clinical governance, not a growth team — the KPI includes the opt-out rate for a reason.

Consent & privacy

Wearable and EHR signals are used only with explicit, revocable consent, privacy-law compliant, and never sold. The patient sees every rule that ever triggers a message.

Equity

Bulk-billing is preserved for concession patients. The demand-triggered $15–25 premium is optional, capped, and applies to non-concession bookings only — a quality signal, not a paywall.

Algorithmic bias

Match suggestions audited quarterly for demographic skew; clinician profiles reviewed at onboarding for anything exclusionary. Patients can always browse and choose freely.

Clinical safety

Cadence rules are derived from published guidelines and signed off by the medical director. Red-flag symptoms route to urgent care instantly — the engine never triages emergencies.
